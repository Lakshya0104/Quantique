Tech Horizon 2.0 · Build & demo plan · Team VOID

VOID-NAV

An instant private network that rescuers carry in, so trapped survivors can call for help when every tower is down.

One scenario, done perfectly: a survivor is trapped (rubble, flood, collapsed building) and still has a phone. A rescuer arrives with a VOID-NAV node. The node creates a local SOS Wi-Fi, the survivor sends an SOS from their phone browser, and the message travels over a self-healing LoRa mesh to a live offline dashboard where rescuers read it, dispatch help, and the status flows back to the survivor.

Instant

Network exists the moment a rescuer switches a node on.

Private

Encrypted, authenticated, no identity collected.

Self-healing

Relays find new routes when one dies.

VOID-NAV · TEAM VOID01
01 · Reality check · how survivors actually join

Nothing connects to a phone by itself. Here's what really happens.

Truth #1: a scanner (ESP32 Wi-Fi/BLE) cannot connect to a phone or push anything onto it. It can only listen. And no node can force a phone to join its Wi-Fi. That's how phones are designed, and it's also a security feature we should be proud of.

Two separate jobs

JobHow it worksNeeds survivor action?
Find phonesESP32 listens for Wi-Fi "probe requests" phones send while searching for networksNo (only Wi-Fi must be ON)
Talk to survivorSurvivor joins open Wi-Fi "VOID-NAV SOS"; SOS page opens automatically (captive portal)Yes: tap the network once

Limits we state honestly

  • Phones with Wi-Fi off, in airplane mode, or dead are invisible.
  • Phones randomise their Wi-Fi ID, so we count devices, never identify them.
  • Bluetooth: most phones don't advertise constantly, so BLE detection is unreliable and a bonus only.
  • Probe frequency drops when screens are off, so detection takes seconds to minutes.

So how will a survivor know to turn on Wi-Fi and join?

Before the disaster

Public awareness: "In a disaster, turn Wi-Fi ON and look for VOID-NAV SOS." Posters at hospitals, schools, panchayats; part of disaster drills (like "Drop, Cover, Hold").

When the alert goes out

The official warning SMS/cell broadcast adds one line: "Keep Wi-Fi ON. Join VOID-NAV SOS if you see it." (proposed integration)

On site

Rescuers shout / use a loudhailer: "Turn on Wi-Fi, join VOID-NAV SOS." Survivors often hear voices before they're found.

Default behaviour

Most people keep Wi-Fi on. An open network named VOID-NAV SOS appears at the top of their Wi-Fi list; some Android versions also notify "open network available".

Judge answer (memorise): "We don't hack or auto-connect to phones; we can't, and we shouldn't. We listen to estimate where phones are, and we invite survivors through an open SOS network that opens a help page automatically. Awareness and on-site calls tell them to join."

03 · SOS input · what goes on the phone

Form first, words optional, meaning always

The SOS page (no OLED needed; the phone is the screen)

  1. Big buttons: Trapped · Injured · Need rescue · Food/water · Safe
  2. Yes / No / Not sure: Anyone injured? Bleeding? Child or elderly with you?
  3. Count: 1 · 2–5 · 6+
  4. Where are you? Ground floor / upper floor / basement / outside (structured)
  5. Optional note (≤ 60 characters): typed in any language
  6. SEND → status shows Sent → Delivered → Read → Dispatched

Push button on the node: one press = instant "Need rescue, unknown details" SOS for someone who can't use a phone or reach the screen.

Should survivors speak (voice)?

Not over the network. Browsers block microphone access on plain local (non-HTTPS) pages, and audio is far too big for LoRa. Workaround that works today: the phone keyboard's mic button (dictation) turns speech into text on many phones. They speak; it types the note.

Is multilingual needed?

Yes, and it's almost free. Buttons are icons + Telugu / Hindi / English labels. Answers are sent as codes, not words, so a Telugu tap shows up in English (or any language) on the dashboard. Free text travels as typed (UTF-8).

Do we need "AI semantic compression"?

For the form: no. The form already is the meaning: "trapped, 3 people, injured, basement" becomes a few bytes of codes. That's true semantic encoding, without any AI. For messy free text: the ESP32 can't run an AI model, so we don't pretend it does. We do two honest things: (1) send the person's words as they are (short, never thrown away), and (2) keyword tagging on the node (e.g. "blood", "khoon", "trapped", "water") that raises priority. Optional at the dashboard: an offline language model on the laptop summarises long notes for rescuers.

How to say it to judges: "We transmit meaning as structured codes, plus the survivor's own short words. We never replace what they said with a guess."

04 · Self-healing mesh

How the relay network repairs itself

LoRa alone is just a radio. "Self-healing" comes from rules we write in firmware.

1 · Hello beacons

Every node broadcasts a tiny "I'm alive, I'm N hops from the gateway" every ~30 s (randomised so beacons don't collide).

2 · Neighbour table

Each node remembers who it heard, their hop count and signal quality. Miss 3 beacons → neighbour removed.

3 · Recompute distance

My hops = best live neighbour + 1. If the old path dies, a new best path appears within ~1–2 beacon periods.

4 · Closer-only forwarding

A node forwards an SOS only if it's closer to the gateway than the sender. Several can qualify, so there are multiple paths by design.

5 · Hop-by-hop receipt + retry

No receipt from the next hop within ~2 s → retry (max 3) → try another neighbour.

6 · Store-and-forward

No route at all? Keep the SOS (urgent first) until a neighbour reappears; drop it only after it expires (30 min).

Anti-jam rules (same as before)

Duplicate IDs dropped · max 4 hops · urgent-first queues · listen-before-talk + random wait · capped retries · beacons slowed when the channel is busy.

Be careful with words

Say "designed for high reliability" (receipts, retries, multiple paths, buffering), not "100 % reliable". Re-route time and delivery rate are to be measured.

05 · Security & privacy

A private network that protects survivors, and can't be abused

Protect the survivor's identity

  • We never collect phone numbers. A Wi-Fi page simply can't read them, and we don't ask.
  • Name is optional. Messages are tied to an anonymous node ID, not a person.
  • Phone counting doesn't store MAC addresses. They're hashed with a salt that changes every hour and deleted after the incident. Phones already randomise them anyway.
  • Local IP addresses are not shown. They're temporary (192.168.4.x) and identify nothing.
  • Data minimisation: keep only what rescuers need; wipe after the operation.

Protect the network itself

  • Encryption on every LoRa packet (AES, per-deployment key loaded by the agency). Eavesdroppers see noise.
  • Authentication tag on every packet: forged or replayed messages are rejected, so no fake SOS floods.
  • Closed network: only nodes holding the deployment key can relay. Random LoRa devices can't join.
  • Dashboard login for rescuers; role-based (operator / viewer).
  • Rate limits per node so one phone can't spam the channel.

On "terrorists use LoRa mesh too": yes, any radio can be misused, and that's exactly why VOID-NAV is closed, authenticated and deployed by authorities, not an open public mesh. Encryption protects survivors from being tracked; the authority holds the keys and the logs. That's the same principle police and army radios already follow.

Survivor-side safety

The SOS page is served by the node, works offline, asks nothing personal, and the open Wi-Fi only reaches the node, not the internet. A scam or fake portal can't hijack the LoRa network without the key.

Radio rules

RA-02 runs at 433 MHz. In India, short-range devices around 433–434 MHz are licence-exempt only at low power, so verify the current WPC limit and run the demo at reduced power (the RA-02 can be set well below 18 dBm).

06 · Quantum · the honest version

Where quantum truly fits, and where it doesn't

Straight answer: the radio, the mesh and the SOS flow are classical, and must be. Microcontrollers in the field can't run quantum programs, and a disaster zone has no internet to reach a quantum computer. Quantum fits in planning and security, done on the command laptop or before deployment.

Will QAOA really work?

Yes, at small scale. We already ran it: 6 candidate sites, place 2 relays, p = 2 → QAOA picked sites 2 + 3 (13 of 21 people), the same answer as checking every option. Simulated circuits handle up to ~20 qubits on a laptop.

The catch: at this size a classical solver is just as fast, and QAOA gives approximate answers. Our dashboard runs both and shows they agree. Pitch it as "the method that scales as quantum hardware grows", not a speed-up today.

08 · Hardware · what we have, what to buy

Designed around 1 ESP32, ready for more

Key fact: a relay needs its own microcontroller and radio. With one ESP32, both RA-02s sit on the same board, so we can show a real over-the-air link in both directions, but not an independent relay. We show relaying/self-healing in a simulator that uses the same packet format, and say so.

You probably don't need it: the ESP32 already scans Wi-Fi probes and BLE. A separate one only helps if it does something extra (e.g. more range).

Balloon demo?

Skip it. Normal (air-filled) balloons don't float. Helium party balloons lift ~10–14 g each; a node (~150–200 g) needs 15–20 of them. Instead, mount the relay high on a pole, tripod or shelf and say "in the field: pole, rooftop or tethered balloon".
