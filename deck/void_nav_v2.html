Team VOID
01 · The problem

Without GPS, every navigation system slowly gets lost, and nobody can tell by how much.

GPS is fragile

Satellite signals are extremely weak by the time they reach the ground. Interference, terrain, tunnels, bunkers, dense forest and underwater operations all cut them off.

The backup drifts

Inertial sensors (accelerometers and gyros) estimate motion, but small errors add up. A low-cost unit can be off by hundreds of metres within minutes.

Good alternatives are expensive

Navigation-grade inertial units and emerging quantum inertial sensors cost lakhs to crores and are export-controlled.

05 · Quantum algorithm 1 · Grover's search

Grover's algorithm finds the matching spot on the map in about √N steps instead of N.

In plain terms

The map is split into 16 cells (4 qubits). The oracle "marks" every cell whose stored field values match what the sensor just measured, within a tolerance. Grover then boosts the marked cells so that measuring the qubits gives the right cell with high probability.

Why it matters

A classical search checks every cell. Grover needs about √N checks: 1 million cells → about 1,000 steps. That is what larger, country-scale maps will need.

Source: adapted from qiskit-community-tutorials/algorithms (Grover), ported to current Qiskit 1.x. At hackathon scale the oracle is built from a classical comparison, so the speed-up is shown in principle, not measured.

06 · Quantum algorithm 2 · Quantum-kernel SVM

A quantum kernel tells apart magnetic signatures that look alike to classical methods.

INPUT FEATURES (PER 1-SECOND WINDOW)
x₁Total field strength |B| (μT)
x₂Vertical component Bz
x₃Change in |B| over the window
x₄Field inclination angle

How it works

Each window of readings is encoded into 4 qubits with a ZZ feature map. The overlap between two encoded states is the "similarity" (kernel). A standard SVM uses that kernel to decide which zone of the map, e.g. corridor A, stairwell, lab, the device is in. This narrows the search before Grover runs.
