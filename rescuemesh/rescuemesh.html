01 · The problem

In a disaster, the network dies first, and the people who need it most are the ones who can't reach anyone.

Earthquakes, floods, cyclones and landslides knock out power and mobile towers within minutes. Survivors often still hold working phones, but with no network those phones are silent.

72 h
The "golden window": survival chances fall sharply the longer someone stays trapped.
0 bars
Towers lose power or backhaul; the networks people depend on vanish exactly when needed.
Blind
Rescuers search building by building with no idea where people are or who is most critical.

Who is affected

  • Survivors: trapped or stranded, unable to say where they are or what they need.
  • Rescue teams (NDRF, SDRF, fire services, Army): spend precious hours searching blindly.
  • Command centres: no live picture of who needs help first.

02 · The solution

RescueMesh: a pocket emergency network that rescuers carry in and drop on site.

Three small battery-powered nodes create a working network in minutes. They find survivors, give them a way to ask for help, and carry those messages kilometres to the command post.

Function 1 · Find

Survivor detection from phone signals

Even with no network, phones keep broadcasting Wi-Fi probe requests and Bluetooth adverts while they search. Node A listens for them and logs signal strength (RSSI) with its GPS position. As the rescuer walks, the dashboard builds a heat map of where phones, and people, probably are.

Function 2 · Connect

"SOS – Rescue Network" hotspot

Node A also opens an open Wi-Fi network. Joining it pops up a one-page help form automatically (captive portal, like hotel Wi-Fi): people count, injuries, needs, a short message. The phone's location is attached. No app, no internet, any phone.

Function 3 · Compress

AI semantic compression

LoRa reaches kilometres but carries only tiny packets. Instead of sending the whole form, an AI step extracts the meaning ("3 people, 1 critical, needs medical + water") and packs it into a 16-byte triage code. This is "semantic communication", a 6G research direction: send the meaning, not the data.

Function 4 · Relay

Self-healing LoRa mesh

Relay nodes on a pole, a helium balloon or a building edge pass packets hop by hop to the command node. If a relay dies, packets reroute. Relay positions are chosen by a quantum optimisation (QAOA) step that maximises how many survivors stay connected.

What command sees

A live map with colour-coded pins (red = critical, amber = urgent, green = stable), the phone-signal heat map, which relay each message came through, and an audible alert for critical cases. All of it works fully offline.

04 · Novelty

What makes RescueMesh different

N-01

Find and talk in one device

Existing tools either locate people (thermal, dogs, radar) or give rescuers comms (sat phones, radios). RescueMesh does both and lets the survivor speak.

N-02

Zero-install for survivors

A captive portal works on any smartphone with no app, account or internet. Most offline-mesh solutions only work if an app was installed beforehand.

N-03

Semantic compression with AI

We send the meaning of a message, not its raw data, a core idea in 6G research. Free-text pleas become structured triage codes that fit long-range, low-bandwidth radio.

N-04

Quantum-optimised relay placement

Choosing where to put a few relays to cover the most survivors is a combinatorial optimisation. We solve it with QAOA on Qiskit and compare against brute force.

N-05

Triage built into the network

Packets carry severity, so the command map and alarms put critical cases first. The network itself does triage.

N-06

Deploy anywhere, cheaply

Under ₹4,000 per kit, battery powered, carried in a backpack, raised on a pole or a helium balloon. No trucks, towers or roads needed.

05 · The quantum part

Where should the relays go? A quantum optimisation answers it.

With only a few relays, where you place them decides how many survivors stay connected. That's a "choose K of N" problem, and it grows explosively as maps get bigger.

How we use QAOA

  1. Input: candidate relay spots (rooftops, poles, open ground) and survivor clusters from the phone-signal heat map, weighted by estimated people.
  2. Formulate: maximise survivors within radio range of a relay, with a penalty if we don't place exactly K relays. This becomes a QUBO, then a cost Hamiltonian HC.
  3. Quantum circuit: one qubit per candidate spot. QAOA alternates the cost layer e−iγHC with a mixer layer e−iβΣX, with p = 2 layers.
  4. Optimise: a classical optimiser (COBYLA) tunes γ and β on Qiskit Aer.
  5. Output: the relay placement, shown on the dashboard map.

Our result (run on Qiskit Aer)

SettingValue
Qubits / candidate relay spots6

Being honest with judges

At 6 spots a laptop solves this instantly by trying every option, so we don't claim a speed-up today. QAOA is the method that scales as maps grow to hundreds of spots and quantum hardware matures. We show it working, verify it against the exact answer, and keep a classical solver as the fallback. Judges respect that honesty.

Survivor detection: honest limits

Modern phones randomise their Wi-Fi MAC address, so we estimate device count and rough location; we never identify people. Signal strength gives distance only roughly (a few metres to tens of metres), so we combine readings from several positions to narrow it down. Phones in airplane mode or switched off can't be found, which is why the SOS hotspot is the second channel.

AI triage: why it matters

Panicked people type messy messages. The AI extracts what rescuers need (how many, how bad, what's needed) and fills the triage fields, so even an unstructured plea becomes a 16-byte, prioritised packet. If the model fails, the form's checkboxes still fill the packet.

09 · Impressing the judges

The 5-minute demo that makes judges remember you

Set-up before judges arrive

  • Hide 2–3 team phones around the area as "trapped survivors", Wi-Fi on.
  • Relay on a balloon or pole at the far end of the hall, or outside the room.
  • Command laptop at your table, Wi-Fi visibly turned off.
  • Node A in a backpack, OLED showing status.

Demo script

  1. Story (30 s): "Earthquake. Towers down. Priya is trapped on the second floor. Her phone works. There's no signal."
  2. Find (60 s): walk with the backpack; the heat map lights up where the hidden phones are.
  3. Connect (60 s): hand the judge your phone. They join "SOS – Rescue Network" and type a message.
  4. Relay (30 s): seconds later a red pin appears on the command map and the alarm beeps.
  5. Resilience (30 s): unplug the relay; show the packet still arriving via the other route, or the queue flushing when it returns.
  6. Quantum + AI (60 s): show the free text turning into a 16-byte code, and QAOA choosing relay spots.

Ten things that win

  1. Make the judge the survivor. Personal involvement beats any slide.
  2. Prove "no internet" by switching Wi-Fi off in front of them.
  3. Show one number: "a message several hundred bytes long becomes 16 bytes."
  4. Show range: the relay is visibly far away.
  5. Lead with people, not tech: start with Priya, not ESP32s.
  1. Be honest about limits: MAC randomisation, RSSI accuracy, quantum scale.
  2. Have a backup video of the full flow, in case the venue is noisy.
  3. Look like a product: boxed nodes, labels, a one-page leaflet.
  4. Know your cost: "a complete kit costs less than one smartphone."
  5. Name the users: NDRF, SDRF, district disaster authorities, Army disaster-relief units.
Pitch line: "When the towers fall, RescueMesh turns every phone into a beacon and every rescuer into a cell tower."

Questions judges will ask, with answers

QuestionAnswer
Isn't scanning phones a privacy problem?We only count and estimate location from randomised, temporary identifiers; nothing is stored beyond the incident; it's opt-in for the SOS channel.
What's the range?LoRa typically reaches hundreds of metres in cities and several kilometres in open line-of-sight; each relay extends it. We'll show a measured number from the venue.
Why not just use satellite?Survivors don't have satellite phones; RescueMesh uses the phones they already carry. A satellite uplink at the command node is a natural extension.
Why quantum?Relay placement is an optimisation that grows explosively with map size; QAOA is a leading quantum method for it. We show it matches the exact answer today and explain where it pays off.
How much battery?An ESP32 + LoRa node runs many hours on a 10,000 mAh power bank; relays can sleep between packets.
Who would buy this?NDRF / SDRF, state disaster authorities, fire services, the Army's disaster-relief units, NGOs and large campuses.
